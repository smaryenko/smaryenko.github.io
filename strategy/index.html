An AI-Era Testing Strategy

Quality isn't a final gate. It's built in from the first line.

AI increases the pace and scale of change. Quality depends on understanding risk, gathering trustworthy evidence and learning from real outcomes. The whole team builds quality; AI accelerates the work; QA connects the context and assures the outcome.

Start here · why bother testing early

A bug gets pricier every step it survives.

Caught in a unit test, a defect costs a coffee break. The same defect caught in production costs a war room. That single curve is the reason for everything that follows.

So the strategy is to catch each defect at the earliest, smallest, cheapest check that can provide enough confidence. The exact multiple is debated; the direction isn't. The right check depends on the risk and the system.

The context · machine-speed change

AI changes the pace of delivery.

AI can understand code, shape requirements, generate changes and tests, and diagnose failures. Speed is the easy win; the bigger one is aiming it at an outcome, not a task.

The catch: without guardrails, AI just ships slop faster — churn that looks like progress and grows technical debt. And as output improves, it stops getting a second look.

Principle · test at the lowest level that gives confidence

Where should we check?

Catch defects as close as practical to their source. Choose the lowest level that gives enough confidence for the risk; the mix of levels depends on the system.

  • Unit — pure logic, one class or function.
  • Component — one deployable service via its API, boundaries mocked.
  • Integration — only when two or more parts together beat isolation.
  • System — the whole thing, end to end.
  • Acceptance — business and regulatory sign-off.

This pyramid is one example, not a required shape or quota. Adapt the levels to architecture and risk; integration-heavy systems may lean more on contracts and component tests.

Principle · combine dimensions of evidence

What should we protect—and how?

Decide where to check, what could go wrong, and how to find out. These choices work together; they are not competing kinds of tests.

1 · WHERE?At what level should we check it? A service, or the whole user journey?
2 · WHAT?What matters most to protect? For example, security, speed, or ease of use.
3 · HOW?How will we find problems? Automated checks, hands-on exploration, or review?
Example · changing sign-in Where: check the sign-in service and the full login journey. What: check account security and whether people can still sign in. How: automate permission checks, then explore unusual user paths.

These examples overlap: security is a concern, exploratory is an approach, and end-to-end describes scope. Give each relevant risk a place in the strategy.

The rule that's easiest to break

Make checks independent.

Keeping tests deterministic and independent sounds simple, until two suites share an environment that never resets. That's where green tests turn flaky. So tests are written to assume the world is already dirty.

Shared counter starts at 7. Run a test:

✕ assert count == 10
✓ assert count increased by 10

Same run, two assertions. The absolute check breaks the moment someone else touched the data; the delta check survives.

  1. Run‑unique IDsnever reuse a fixed record two suites both write to.
  2. Delta, not absolutecapture state at start, assert on the change you made.
  3. Clean what you canrecords, artifacts, config overrides, all torn down.
  4. Never touch productiontest data stays out of prod, full stop.

The other half of a trustworthy test

Build for observability.

A feature isn't fully testable until it exposes enough evidence to prove it worked, in a test and later in production. Observability is a requirement of the feature, not an afterthought.

Good observability pays off three times: reliable automated checks, faster diagnosis, and safer canaries. It is also what lets AI own an outcome rather than a task: it can only keep a journey healthy if it can see, reproduce and verify.

Past the gate · release is the start, not the end

Release in stages. Learn from real outcomes.

Test environments never match production, so some defects only show under real traffic. Expose the change to a small slice, watch the signals, then promote it — or roll it back before most users notice.

Example · scale AI controls with autonomy and risk

Bound AI autonomy by risk.

As AI takes on more consequential actions, require stronger controls. Adapt the rungs and safeguards to the task and its risk.

Every rung needs data guardrails: approved models, repo and ticket content treated as untrusted, and auditable decisions. And every rung assumes people delegate the work but keep ownership of the result: AI output stays a hypothesis to check, never an answer accepted unread.

Ownership at machine scale

Everyone builds quality.
QA owns the release verdict.

QA does not review every generated line. It connects context to independent evidence and decides whether the product is ready.

The whole team builds it

  • Engineering designs and automates for quality
  • Product defines outcomes and residual risk
  • Security and operations own specialised controls and signals
→

QA owns assurance

  • Connects customer, business and technical context to critical expectations
  • Assesses evidence against risk; unknowns are not passes
  • Owns the verdict, can explain it, and blocks release when evidence falls short

Automation may pause or roll back against pre-agreed thresholds.

Why independence matters: if only admins may delete a record, write that expectation first. Otherwise, AI can allow any signed-in user and generate a test that confirms the same mistake—green, but wrong.
